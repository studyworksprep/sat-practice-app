## The convention (plan 3.5)

A lesson's intro video is **60–90 seconds**, and it is **block 1** —
before any teaching. Face plus whiteboard. Three beats, in order:

1. **Name the move.** Say the name the lesson uses, in the words a tutor
   says in session. The name is the thing students carry out of the video.
2. **The test-day payoff.** What this buys them in the two minutes they
   get per question — time saved, a trap dodged, an algebra step skipped.
3. **One caution.** Exactly one. The failure that actually happens, not a
   list of everything that can go wrong.

Nothing else: no agenda, no "in this lesson we will," no summary of the
block list. The lesson does the teaching; the video makes a student want
to start it and gives them the vocabulary to hang it on.

Authoring notes: add the `video` block only once the recording exists
(never invent a URL — see the authoring guide's video section); unlisted
YouTube links embed as-is, and an unlisted Vimeo link must keep its
privacy hash. Give the block a one-line `caption` naming the move.

**Solve Equations by Graphing: Find the x-Intercepts.** The move is
*zero on one side, then click the intercepts*. Whatever the equation
looks like — fractions, radicals, absolute value, a quartic — you push
everything to one side, graph what's left, and every place the curve
touches the x-axis is a solution. Payoff: you never have to recognize
what *kind* of equation it is, and you get *all* the solutions, which is
what trips people on "how many solutions" questions. Caution: when the
choices are radicals or fractions and your intercept is a decimal, don't
eyeball the match — type the choice into the calculator and compare
decimals.

**Find a Standard Regression Equation from Data.** The move is *points
in a table, then the Regression button*. Data can arrive as a table, a
graph, or a sentence in a word problem; your only job is to turn it into
input-output pairs, type them in, and let Desmos produce the equation.
Payoff: you generate the answer instead of testing four choices, and it
works the same for linear, quadratic, and exponential. Caution: the
order of the pair is the whole game — the input goes in the left column.
"After 2 hours there were 180 bacteria" is (2, 180), never (180, 2).

**Solve Systems of Equations by Graphing.** The move is *type both
equations exactly as written and click where they cross*. No
substitution, no elimination, no rearranging into y = form. Payoff: on
essentially every SAT system, this converts a multi-step algebra problem
into two lines of typing — and it can't go wrong the way a sign slip in
elimination can. Caution: after you click the intersection, read the
question again. It often wants *x + y*, or just *y*, not the point you
just clicked.

**Special Systems: No Solution and Infinitely Many Solutions.** The move
is *same slope decides it*. Parallel lines never meet, so no solution;
identical lines overlap everywhere, so infinitely many. Payoff: these
questions look like they need heavy algebra to find a mystery constant,
and instead they collapse to matching one slope. Caution: the slider is
for *seeing* it, not for answering — at A = 1.9 and A = 2 the lines look
identical on screen. Estimate with the slider, then confirm with the
slope or the a-to-b ratio.

**Find the Equation with My Numbers.** The move is *build one honest
situation from the story, then test the choices*. Pick easy values that
obey the problem, work out what actually happens, and see which equation
tells that same story. Payoff: you skip the translation step where most
of the errors live — you never have to write the equation, only
recognize it. Caution: build your numbers from the story *before* you
look at the equations. Reading the choices first quietly bends your
numbers toward one of them.

**Factor Out the Greatest Common Factor.** The move is *pull out the
biggest shared piece — number and variables — then check what's left*.
Payoff: it's the first step of nearly every factoring problem on the
test, and it turns a scary polynomial into a short one. Caution:
"factored" and "fully factored" are different answers. After you pull
something out, look inside the parentheses again — if the terms still
share a factor, you're not done, and that half-factored form is sitting
right there in the choices.

**Factor Polynomials: Trinomials and Difference of Squares.** The move is
*factoring is multiplication run backwards*. Find the pair that
multiplies to the last term and adds to the middle one; recognize
difference of squares on sight. Payoff: two patterns cover most of what
the SAT asks, and knowing *why* they work means you can rebuild them
under pressure instead of recalling a rule. Caution: not everything
factors. When no integer pair works, "prime" is a real answer — don't
invent a pair because the directions said to factor.

**Recognize and Use Similar Triangles.** The move is *match the angles,
then rank the sides*. Equal angles tell you which vertices correspond;
smallest side matches smallest, largest matches largest. Payoff: you
stop mentally rotating triangles — which is where the errors come from —
and the proportion writes itself. Caution: size ranking is a
cross-check, not proof. The angles decide the correspondence, especially
when the figure says "not drawn to scale."

**Rates in Two-Variable Equations: Read the Units.** The move is *every
number carries a unit, so read the units to read the meaning*. Payoff:
the arithmetic in these questions is trivial; what's actually tested is
whether you can say what a coefficient, a product, or the total *means*
— and units answer that directly. Caution: the coefficient isn't always
the rate. Check what the variable counts first, because 9r means
something different depending on whether r is hours or trips.

**Find Probability from Tables: Favorable over Total.** The move is
*Total first*. Decide what group you're drawing from before you count
anything favorable. Payoff: nearly every wrong answer on these items is
a right count over the wrong total, so fixing the denominator first
removes the trap the test is actually setting. Caution: "given that"
restricts your total — and it can name either a row or a column, so read
which category it's actually naming.

**Understand and Use Standard Deviation.** The move is *standard
deviation is a spread meter*. It measures typical distance from the
mean; you compare it by eye, you don't compute it. Payoff: the SAT asks
you to compare two sets, or say what happens when a value moves — all
answerable from the picture, with no formula. Caution: a bigger mean does
not mean a bigger standard deviation. Compare each set's spread around
its *own* center.

**Solve SAT Boundaries Questions in the Fastest Order.** The move is
*fixed order: full stop, colon, dash, comma*. Same order every question.
Payoff: you stop reading four choices and asking which sounds best —
sound is exactly what the test exploits — and you get a mechanical
routine that runs the same way under time pressure. Caution: when two
choices do the identical job, neither can be right. Cross them both off
before you think any harder.

**Subject–Verb Agreement: Odd-One-Out.** The move is *test each choice
after "he" and after "they"*. When exactly one choice is singular and
three are plural, or the reverse, the odd one out is the answer.
Payoff: you answer without finding the subject or untangling the
sentence — which is where the test hides its interrupting phrases.
Caution: check first that the four choices actually differ in
singular/plural. If they differ in tense, this trick doesn't apply and
you have to read the sentence.

**Rhetorical Synthesis: Let the Goal Lead.** The move is *turn the goal
into a requirement list, then eliminate*. The notes look like the task;
the student's stated goal is the actual decision-maker. Payoff: most
choices are perfectly true statements drawn from the notes, so "is this
true?" separates nothing — goal-fit separates everything. Caution: when
the goal has two parts joined by "and," treat it as a contract. A choice
that nails one half and drops the other is out.

**Process and Pre-Answer Reading Comprehension Questions.** The move is
*form your answer before you look at theirs*. Name the question's job,
build a usable read of the passage, then say what the answer should be
in your own words. Payoff: four polished choices are designed to look
reasonable one at a time; arriving with a target means you're matching
instead of being persuaded. Caution: your target is a rough idea in your
own words. Don't polish it into something that sounds like an answer
choice — that's how you talk yourself into one.

**Good Cop / Bad Cop: Prove Every Reading Answer.** The move is *two
passes per choice*: good cop notices what matches, bad cop hunts for the
one word that makes it wrong. Payoff: it makes elimination evidence-based
instead of vibe-based, and it catches the choice that is 90% right — the
one that costs the most points. Caution: strong words aren't
automatically wrong. Don't cross out "all" or "never" on sight; compare
the strength of the choice against the strength of the text.

**Use My Numbers to Make Abstract SAT Problems Concrete.** The move is
*replace open variables with easy numbers you choose*. When variables
can take many valid values, pick friendly ones that obey every
condition, solve your concrete version, and test the choices. Payoff:
the algebra you were about to do disappears; you trade it for arithmetic
you can trust. Caution: if the problem ties two variables together, you
only get to choose one of them — the other is calculated, never picked.

**Solve Equations with Regression in Desmos.** The move is *swap one
equals sign for a tilde and read what Desmos assigns*. Payoff: it solves
equations you'd otherwise have to rearrange, and it extends to small
systems once you add a definition line. Caution: one assigned value is
one solution, not every solution. When the question asks how many
solutions exist, switch to graphing.

**Find Missing Constants with Custom Regression.** The move is *type the
equation form the question requires, and let Desmos fill in the
constant*. Extra information becomes a definition line like `a = 10 − b`.
Payoff: this is the power move for "the graph passes through these
points and has this form" — no system-solving by hand. Caution: Desmos
only assigns the constants it has freedom to fit. If a definition
supplies one of them, that one comes from your definition, not from the
readout.

**Find Missing Constants in Equivalent Expressions with Regression.** The
move is *define the input as a list of allowed values, then regress*.
Payoff: "equivalent for every value of x" questions become a fit instead
of an expansion, so no sign slips. Caution: if the regression panel
starts assigning a value to your *input* variable, you forgot to define
it as a list — that's the tell.

**Testing Equivalent Expressions with Desmos Sliders.** The move is
*match it, don't expand it*. Give each variable a slider, pick random
values, and see which choice produces the same number. Payoff: multi-
variable "which is equivalent" questions stop being sign-error
minefields. Caution: at the default slider values, several wrong choices
can agree with the original by coincidence. Move the sliders and test a
second set before you commit.

**Functions and Function Notation on the SAT.** The move is *read
function notation as information*: an input produces an output, and a
pair is a point on a graph. Payoff: `f(3) = 6` stops being notation to
decode and becomes a point you can use, graph, or solve from. Caution:
when a condition determines a missing constant, check the stated
condition against your answer — a fit can return one of two values, and
only one satisfies what the problem said.

**Solve Percent and Percent Change Problems with Desmos.** The move is
*translate the sentence, then let regression solve it* — Desmos writes
"of" for you as soon as you type a percent sign. Payoff: percent-change
wording is where people invert the base; typing the sentence nearly
verbatim keeps the base where the sentence put it. Caution: the value
after "than" is the old value, and it's the basis for the comparison.
Get that backwards and every step after it is wrong.

**Use Desmos Lists and List Tools.** The move is *store the values once
and operate on the whole list*. Payoff: add a constant to every value,
combine two groups, or take a mean without retyping anything. Caution:
the Bluebook calculator is not the full Desmos — `repeat` isn't there.
Stick to what the testing calculator actually has.

**Advanced Factoring: Non-Monic Trinomials and Cubes.** The move is
*structured trial and error driven by reverse multiplication* — the
outside-inside products have to add to the middle term. Payoff: a
leading coefficient other than 1 stops being a wall. Caution: before you
grind, ask whether the question actually needs a factored form. If it
asks for solutions, graphing may be faster.

**Use Scale Factors with Similar Shapes.** The move is *one linear scale
factor, three different powers*: lengths by k, areas by k², volumes by
k³. Payoff: any one measurement gets you to any other, in both
directions. Caution: never cube an area ratio. Go back to the linear
scale factor first, then raise it.

**Right Triangle Trigonometry with SOHCAHTOA.** The move is *sketch it
and label from the angle you're using*. The SAT names vertices, not
"opposite" and "adjacent," so the sketch does the translating. Payoff:
once labeled, choosing sine, cosine, or tangent is mechanical. Caution:
"adjacent" never means the hypotenuse, even though the hypotenuse also
touches the angle — and check that the calculator is in degrees.

**Special Right Triangles: Recognize, Scale, Solve.** The move is *see
the triangle before the angles appear*. A square's diagonal, an
equilateral triangle cut in half, "isosceles right triangle" — none of
them print 45 or 30, and all of them are special triangles. Payoff: one
side gives you all three, instantly. Caution: the ratio is fixed but the
size isn't. Find the scale factor before you write any side down.

**Circle Toolkit: Measure, Arcs, and Equations.** The move is *find the
radius or the center first, then pick the fact*. Circumference, area,
arc, tangent, equation — they all hang off that one measurement.
Payoff: a scattered-looking topic collapses into one opening step.
Caution: only the area formula squares the radius. That's the swap the
test is counting on.

**Reason Through SAT Survey Questions.** The move is *check the sample,
then limit the conclusion to the population it came from*. Payoff: these
questions look statistical but are almost entirely about scope — who was
asked, and who you're therefore allowed to talk about. Caution: margin
of error measures sampling uncertainty only. It does not certify the
survey was unbiased, and it doesn't guarantee the true value.

**Place Transition Words by Logic.** The move is *decide which two ideas
the transition connects, then place it*. Punctuation alone won't
separate "transition before the period" from "transition after it."
Payoff: it settles the choices that are all grammatically legal.
Caution: a sentence absolutely can end with a transition — don't
eliminate one just because it's sitting at the end.

**Bracket the Pivot: Choose Precise SAT Transitions.** The move is
*bracket the two ideas, shrink each to a short statement, predict the
relationship, then match*. Payoff: unfamiliar research or art topics stop
mattering — you're comparing two small logical statements, not becoming
an expert. Caution: don't start with the answer choices. Four polished
transition words become four competing interpretations if you haven't
predicted first.

**Initial Modifiers: Match the Noun After the Comma.** The move is *ask
what the opening phrase describes, and put that noun right after the
comma*. Payoff: one question answers the item, without untangling four
rewritten sentences. Caution: ask the question before you read the
choices. Reading them first makes several sound acceptable.

**Words in Context: Read, Predict, Match.** The move is *read, predict,
then match* — decide what the blank must mean before seeing any word.
Payoff: the choices are all real words that fit the grammar; only a
prediction separates them. Caution: the blank's sentence tells you where
the answer goes, but the whole text tells you what it means. If nothing
matches your prediction, make a second pass rather than settling.

**Inference Questions: Make the Smallest Supported Leap.** The move is
*soundbite each sentence, combine them, take the smallest step*.
Payoff: it stops you reaching for the interesting conclusion, which is
almost always the wrong one. Caution: don't add new dots. Every part of
the answer has to come from the text, even when a bigger claim feels
obviously true.

**CLEAR the Claim: Command of Evidence.** The move is *predict the
evidence the claim needs before reading the choices*. Payoff: evidence
questions become a match rather than a scavenger hunt through four
plausible quotations. Caution: with a graph, read the labels first. A
bar's height means nothing until you know what the axis is counting.
